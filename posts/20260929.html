Fretboard Codex has grown quite a bit this week. What began as an interactive way to explore scales, modes, chords, voicings, and modal interchange now also lets you turn those ideas into music.

The new Compose workspace lets you build a progression from diatonic and borrowed chords, rearrange it, set a tempo, and loop playback while the fretboard lights up with each chord’s tones. You can inspect playable shapes, including drop-2 voicings, and share a link to a progression that transposes when you change key. The core experience still works offline once the app has been installed.

I also simplified the site around Explore and Compose, refreshed its After Osmosis day and night styling, and made the layout easier to use on a phone. Fretboard Codex has moved to Cloudflare hosting, and optional accounts now let you save sketches across devices. You can sign in with Google or email, export your saved library, and delete your account from the site.

There’s more I want to explore, especially richer voicings and ways to make saved sketches more useful. For now, I’d love to hear what you make with it: try Fretboard Codex.
